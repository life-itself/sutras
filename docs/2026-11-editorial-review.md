# Editorial review of the eight editions

Status: working file, 2026-11-20, editorial track (bead sutras-m2v.5). Brief: [3-editorial.md](plans/workstreams/3-editorial.md); voice: [brand.md](brand.md). The eight editions were each written by a different agent and critiqued once; this is the first read of them as a set. Rufus has not yet reviewed any of them. The review page for him is linked at the end.

What the brief asks of an introduction: say what the text is, why read it, and how to read it, in 150–300 words; plain, quiet, personal; British spelling; no praise adjectives; every fact from a source that was read; doubt about attribution said plainly; signed as editorial.

## 1. Each text against the brief

Word counts are of the introduction only (in brief excluded), before editing.

### Faith in Mind (258 words)

Does well: the model for the set. What it is (a short Ch’an poem, 146 lines), who tradition gives it to and why that is doubted (with Sheng-yen as the source), the theme in its first two lines, how Sheng-yen used it, one way to read it, and where the notes stand. Every fact traced to Sheng-yen's introduction or the book's front matter.
Does badly: little. "Who wrote it is not known" repeats "The attribution is doubtful". The header lacks the "Sutras" kicker link the other seven have (template, not editorial; noted for the site track).

### Shodoka (310 words)

Does well: the one-night story, Hu Shih's two arguments against the attribution (named and dated), the contrast with *Faith in Mind* ("Where *Faith in Mind* is quiet and even, this is a song"), the claim in the first verse, how to read it.
Does badly: ten words over; "full of lions, jewels and moons in water" is colour the reader can find for themselves; "it was first printed in a book of sutras for chanting" overstates the source, which says the translation was made for the Diamond Sangha and later reprinted in *Daily Sutras for Chanting and Recitation*. The second paragraph does three jobs (doubt, Sheng-yen's regard, the Japanese set of four) and is the longest in the set.

### The Diamond Sutra, Thich Nhat Hanh (312 words)

Does well: the scene (the Buddha washes his feet and sits down), the sutra's one move said plainly ("what is called X is not X"), the translator's own advice on how to read it, the link to the other translation with matching section numbers.
Does badly: twelve words over; the first paragraph is reference-book (Sanskrit title, Chinese title, Perfection of Wisdom, Kumārajīva, Dunhuang) before anything a reader could care about; "Taishō 235" in the third paragraph belongs under About this translation; "the oldest dated printed book we have" is true but a museum fact, not a reason to read.

### The Diamond Sutra, Red Pine (300 words)

Does well: honest about being the second translation and pointing to the first for the sutra itself; Red Pine's own change of mind ("It turns out the sutra isn’t about emptiness"), which is a real reason to read this version; "We find Thich Nhat Hanh’s version easier going" is the kind of plain reader's judgement the brand asks for.
Does badly: two long quotations make up a third of it; the editions used (Müller 1881, Conze 1957) are apparatus; nothing on what the sutra says, only on how Red Pine read it.

### On the Shortness of Life (289 words)

Does well: who, to whom, when and why the date is uncertain, the claim in the first chapter, the shape of the argument, the one Latin word worth knowing (*occupati*), what is Roman and what is not ("Very little of the argument is"), how to read it, what the notes are. The best balance of the eight after *Faith in Mind*.
Does badly: "statesman and playwright" is a CV; "The busy man of chapter 7 who cries out" is fine, but the quotation is the text's, so it should link to the chapter.

### The Gateless Gate (286 words)

Does well: what a koan is in one line, the three parts of a case, the first case given in full as the way in, Mumon's own refusal to explain, how to read (one case a day, then leave it alone).
Does badly: "prods and teases" is a touch of colour; the paragraph on the book's history in China and Japan is from Wikipedia and could be shorter; the naming (Wumen / Mumon, Longxiang / Ryūshō) takes a lot of the first paragraph.

### Dhammapada (414 words)

Does well: the teaching located in the first pair of verses, with the ox-cart and the shadow; the translator's reasons for free verse and for "heart".
Does badly: a third too long. The third paragraph retells the translator's introduction (the two paths, "further entrapment") in his sermon register rather than ours; "has long been recognized as one of the masterpieces of early Buddhist literature" is a praise quotation; "though, as the translator notes, it is by no means elementary" is hedging; the fifth paragraph is about where the notes sit and which readers the translator's apparatus is for, which belongs under About this translation. No date at all for the text (the frontmatter has one, from the translator's historical notes). The in brief ends with a feature ("so you can link to any one of them").

### Meditations (234 words)

Does well: what it is and is not (notes to himself; the title is not his), Hadot's "spiritual exercises", Book 1 as the exception, two short entries to start with.
Does badly: no sentence on why read it beyond Hays's two themes; "Hays says", "Hays calls", "Hays picks out", "Hays finds" four times in three paragraphs, so it reads as a summary of his introduction rather than as ours. Left as it stands until the Meditations session has finished (its tmux session was still running on 2026-11-20).

## 2. Across the set

**Structure.** All eight have the same six parts in the same order, the same signature ("Editors’ introduction, drafted with an AI assistant. November 2026.") and the same note label ("*Editors’ note.*"). The heading over the text varies by kind (The poem, The sutra, The essay, The text, The verses): deliberate and kept.

**Reading time.** Each text says how long it is in a different way: "A few minutes to read" (Faith in Mind), "about an hour" (Seneca), "can be read in half an hour" (Red Pine, quoting him), "about 6,000 words" (Diamond), "about 10,000 words of verse" (Dhammapada), "about 35,000 words" (Meditations), nothing (Shodoka, Mumonkan). Decision: every in brief ends with the time to read straight through, in the same form, and word counts go. Counted from the files: Faith in Mind 885 words, Shodoka 2,570, Diamond 5,787, Red Pine 7,252, Mumonkan 8,849 (English), Dhammapada 10,431, Seneca 10,666, Meditations 34,493.

**Names.** Three Chinese-tradition editions romanise three ways: *Faith in Mind* follows Sheng-yen's unhyphenated Wade-Giles ("Seng Ts’an", "Hui Neng"); the Shodoka hyphenates ("Hui-neng") and gives pinyin once; the Mumonkan uses pinyin for Chinese names and Japanese readings for the translation's. Decision: our prose follows the translator's forms in each edition, so the page reads as one; the pinyin goes in brackets at a person's first mention in the introduction, so the texts can be connected; "Hui-neng" is hyphenated wherever it is ours. "Pāli" with the macron in our prose (the Mumonkan note had "Pali").

**Numbers.** "64 verses" and "423 verses" in digits; "forty-eight koans", "twenty-six chapters", "twelve books" in words. Decision: counts of passages, verses, chapters, cases and books in digits; other small numbers in words.

**Dates.** "d. 606" (frontmatter) against "died in 606" (prose); "c. 4 BCE" against "about 4 BCE". Decision: "about" in prose, "c." only in frontmatter; "CE" on a first date where the century is not obvious, then dropped.

**Doubt about attribution.** Said the same way in both doubtful cases ("Tradition gives it to…, though he probably did not write it"), and "(attributed; doubtful)" in both author fields. The Dhammapada said nothing about how the verses reached us; one sentence from the translator's historical notes is added.

**Copyright.** Five editions use the *Faith in Mind* wording word for word. Seneca and Meditations drop "or taken down" (right for Seneca, which is probably public domain in the US; an oversight for Meditations). The list of "our own additions" differs: the Seneca lists About this translation, the Meditations the contents list, the Dhammapada leaves out the notes. Decision: the list names what we actually wrote on that page; Meditations gets "or taken down" back when its session ends. The Mumonkan frontmatter licence names no holder ("Translation © 1977"): left, because the source page does not say who holds it, and the prose says so.

**Translators' notes.** Seneca's and Hays's notes are unlabelled; the Dhammapada's are labelled "*Translator’s note on 1-2.*" because they were end notes moved to their verses and name the verses they discuss. Kept; the About section of each says whose the unlabelled notes are.

**Download line.** Four different descriptions of what the EPUB holds. Not changed here: what each EPUB actually contains is the site track's to state (bead filed if the wording is wrong).

**Cross-links.** Faith in Mind ↔ Shodoka, Diamond ↔ Red Pine, Mumonkan → Shodoka. Added: Seneca → Meditations (Meditations → Seneca when that session ends).

**Spelling.** A scan of the editorial prose of all eight (in brief, introduction, About lead, Source and licence, editors' notes, quotations excluded) found no American spellings.

## 4. Editors' highlights

Chosen 2026-11-20, three to eight a text, each quoted verbatim and checked by `scripts/notes` to occur exactly once in its passage. They live as data in `annotations/<slug>.yaml`, marked `by: Editors` (decision 0002's format), separate from Rufus's own notes, and show in the margin with a ※ mark. A first pass was proposed by one subagent per text with the brand brief in hand; the editor cut, reworded and sourced the notes (one claim about Hui-neng and section 10 of the Diamond Sutra was dropped for want of a source read). Meditations' eight are listed here and not yet in `annotations/meditations.yaml`, which is Rufus's file; they go in when the Meditations session has finished.


**Faith in Mind** (`annotations/faith-in-mind.yaml`)

- [1](/faith-in-mind#1): “The Supreme Way is not difficult If only you do not pick and choose.”
- [2](/faith-in-mind#2): “Stop activity and return to stillness, And that stillness will be even more active.” — Stillness is not the resting place here. Stanza 10 goes further: when activity stops, there is no rest either.
- [3](/faith-in-mind#3): “No need to seek the real; Just extinguish your views.”
- [4](/faith-in-mind#4): “Two comes from one, Yet do not even keep the one.” — Oneness has been the poem’s word for the Way until now; here even that is let go. Stanza 10 asks the same thing the other way round: if two cannot be established, how can there be one?
- [5](/faith-in-mind#5): “The Great Way is broad, Neither easy nor difficult.” — The first line said the Way is not difficult. Here it is neither easy nor difficult.
- [7](/faith-in-mind#7): “Using mind to cultivate mind — Is this not a great mistake?”
- [14](/faith-in-mind#14): “Faith and mind are not two; Non-duality is faith in mind.” — The title arrives here, in the last full stanza. In his introduction below, Sheng-yen gives the phrase two senses: believing in the mind and realising it.
- [15](/faith-in-mind#15): “The path of words is cut off; There is no past, no future, no present.”

**Shodoka** (`annotations/shodoka.yaml`)

- [1](/shodoka#1): “The real nature of ignorance is the Buddha-nature itself; The empty delusory body is the very body of the…”
- [10](/shodoka#10): “It is not difficult to see images in a mirror, But who can take hold of the moon in the water?” — The moon in water comes back in verse 36: one moon, reflected in many waters.
- [19](/shodoka#19): “Walking is Zen, sitting is Zen; Speaking or silent, active or quiet, the essence is at peace.”
- [28](/shodoka#28): “The awakened one does not seek truth — Does not cut off delusion.” — The first verse said the same. So does [Faith in Mind](/faith-in-mind#3): “No need to seek the real; / Just extinguish your views.”
- [39](/shodoka#39): “If you search elsewhere, you cannot see it. You cannot grasp it, you cannot reject it; In the midst of not…”
- [52](/shodoka#52): “I entered the sea to count the sands in vain And then the Tathagata scolded me kindly As I read “What…” — The one place the singer tells his own story as a scholar. The other is verse 18, where the wandering ends at Ts’ao Ch’i.
- [64](/shodoka#64): “Don’t belittle the sky by looking through a pipe. If you still don’t understand, I will settle it for you.”

**The Diamond Sutra (Thich Nhat Hanh)** (`annotations/diamond.yaml`)

- [3](/diamond#3): “And when this innumerable, immeasurable, infinite number of beings has become liberated, we do not, in…” — The whole sutra is in this sentence. When Subhuti asks his question a second time, in section 17, the Buddha answers with it again, almost word for word.
- [5](/diamond#5): “In a place where there is something that can be distinguished by signs, in that place there is deception.…”
- [6](/diamond#6): “This is the hidden meaning when the Tathagata says, ‘Bhikshus, you should know that all of the teachings I…”
- [10](/diamond#10): “They should give rise to an intention with their minds not dwelling anywhere.” — Red Pine, from the Sanskrit, has “a thought that is not attached” ([/diamond-red-pine#10](/diamond-red-pine#10)).
- [14](/diamond#14): “Buddhas are called Buddhas because they are free of ideas.”
- [18](/diamond#18): “Subhuti, the past mind cannot be grasped, neither can the present mind or the future mind.” — After five eyes and all the sand in the Ganges, the section ends on this. Red Pine has “thought” for “mind” ([/diamond-red-pine#18](/diamond-red-pine#18)).
- [27](/diamond#27): “Do not think that when one gives rise to the highest, most fulfilled, awakened mind, one needs to see all…” — Having taken everything away, the sutra stops the reader drawing the obvious conclusion. Not-X is not nothing.
- [32](/diamond#32): “All composed things are like a dream, a phantom, a drop of dew, a flash of lightning. That is how to…”

**The Diamond Sutra (Red Pine)** (`annotations/diamond-red-pine.yaml`)

- [3](/diamond-red-pine#3): “And though I thus liberate countless beings, not a single being is liberated.”
- [6](/diamond-red-pine#6): “A dharma teaching is like a raft. If you should let go of dharmas, how much more so no dharmas.” — Thich Nhat Hanh ends the same saying: “All teachings must be abandoned, not to mention non-teachings.” ([/diamond#6](/diamond#6))
- [9](/diamond-red-pine#9): “For he dwells nowhere at all.” — Thich Nhat Hanh has Subhuti dwelling in “the concentration of peaceful abiding” ([/diamond#9](/diamond#9)). The Sanskrit Red Pine follows has him dwelling nowhere.
- [14](/diamond-red-pine#14): “Those beings shall be most remarkably blessed, Subhuti, who are not alarmed, not frightened, and not…”
- [18](/diamond-red-pine#18): “Subhuti, a past thought cannot be found. A future thought cannot be found. Nor can a present thought be found.”
- [26](/diamond-red-pine#26): “Who looks for me in form who seeks me in a voice indulges in wasted effort such people see me not.”
- [28](/diamond-red-pine#28): “They would, Subhuti, but without grasping it. Thus is it called ‘obtaining.’” — The merit is kept; only the having is let go. Thich Nhat Hanh puts the same exchange as not needing to “build up virtue and happiness” ([/diamond#28](/diamond#28)).
- [32](/diamond-red-pine#32): “And how should they explain it? By not explaining. Thus is it called ‘explaining.’” — Thich Nhat Hanh answers the same question at length ([/diamond#32](/diamond#32)): “Without being caught up in signs, just according to things as they are, without agitation.”

**On the Shortness of Life** (`annotations/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life.yaml`)

- [1](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#1): “It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it.”
- [3](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#3): “You have all the fears of mortals and all the desires of immortals.”
- [7](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#7): “There is nothing the busy man is less busied with than living: there is nothing that is harder to learn.” — The busy man is one of the *occupati*, “the engrossed” in Basore’s word, the people this whole essay is about.
- [9](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#9): “The greatest hindrance to living is expectancy, which depends upon the morrow and wastes to-day. You…” — Marcus Aurelius says the same in his own notes ([Meditations 3.10](/meditations#3.10)): “Each of us lives only now, this brief instant.”
- [10](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#10): “Life is divided into three periods—that which has been, that which is, that which will be. Of these the…” — This reverses the usual complaint. The past, not the future, is the part we securely own, because Fortune has finished with it; chapters 14 and 15 build on this.
- [14](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#14): “Since Nature allows us to enter into fellowship with every age, why should we not turn from this paltry…”
- [15](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#15): “He makes his life long by combining all times into one.” — The three periods of chapter 10, gathered up: the past by recollection, the present by use, the future by anticipation.
- [19](/seneca-on-the-shortness-of-life#19): “If these wish to know how short their life is, let them reflect how small a part of it is their own.”

- [23](/gateless-gate-mumonkan#23): “What I have told you is no secret at all. When you look into your own true self, whatever is deeper is…”
- [29](/gateless-gate-mumonkan#29): “It is not the wind that moves; it is not the flag that moves; it is not the mind that moves.” — The case ends with the patriarch’s answer: it is your mind that moves. Mumon’s comment opens by taking that away too.
- [37](/gateless-gate-mumonkan#37): “A monk asked Jōshū, “What is the meaning of Bodhidharma’s coming to China?” Jōshū said, “The oak tree in…”
- [postscript](/gateless-gate-mumonkan#postscript): “Your direct realization is demanded; it should not be sought through others.” — Mumon’s own instruction for reading his book, written after the 48 cases.

**Dhammapada** (`annotations/dhammapada.yaml`)

- [1](/dhammapada#1): “Phenomena are preceded by the heart, ruled by the heart, made of the heart.” — The whole book rests on this: what you meet is shaped by the state you meet it in. Everything after is about tending that state.
- [80](/dhammapada#80): “Carpenters shape the wood. The wise control themselves.”
- [121](/dhammapada#121): “A water jar fills, even with water falling in drops.” — Said twice, once of harm and once of good (verse 122). The small habit is the whole argument.
- [183](/dhammapada#183): “the cleansing of one’s own mind: this is the teaching of the Awakened.”
- [201](/dhammapada#201): “The calmed lie down with ease, having set winning & losing aside.”
