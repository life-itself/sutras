## Introduction

The Dhammapada is an anthology of verses attributed to the Buddha. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, who translated it, writes that it “has long been recognized as one of the masterpieces of early Buddhist literature.” It has been used for centuries as a first introduction to Buddhism.

Its first two verses give the whole idea. “Phenomena are preceded by the heart, ruled by the heart, made of the heart.” If you speak or act with a corrupted heart, suffering follows you, as the wheel follows the track of the ox that pulls the cart. If you speak or act with a calm, bright heart, happiness follows you “like a shadow / that never leaves.” The rest of the book comes back to this one choice.

The first three chapters set out two ways of living. The wise train the mind, and the fool sees no reason to. The later chapters follow both paths. The wise path can lead to happiness within the cycle of death and rebirth, or all the way to the Deathless, beyond it. The fool’s path leads to suffering now and in the future, and to further entrapment. The translator says the purpose of the book is to make the wise path attractive, so that the reader follows it.

Thanissaro reads the book as poetry as well as teaching. He points out that it belongs to the Indian tradition of *kavya*, where a work was meant “to instruct in the highest ends of life while simultaneously giving delight.” His translation is in free verse, which he chose because it needs the fewest departures from the literal sense and allows a terse directness. It keeps the word *mano* as “heart” in the first verses, following a Thai tradition, as the note at verse 1 explains.

The translator’s notes sit at the verses they discuss, as margin notes where the screen allows. Each one is labelled with the verse or verses it is about. The book’s own introduction is for readers who want a guide to conduct; its historical notes are for readers who want to read the text in the history of Buddhism. The notes are for both. A reasonable way in is one chapter at a time, a verse at a time, with the notes at the verses that catch your attention.
